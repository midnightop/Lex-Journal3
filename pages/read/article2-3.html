Foundations of the 10th Schedule

The Indian political landscape is often defined by a delicate balance between party loyalty and individual ambition. Introduced by the 52nd Amendment in 1985, the Anti-Defection Law was a desperate legislative response to the "Aaya Ram Gaya Ram" culture that plagued the 1960s and 70s. Its primary objective was to curb political instability caused by legislators switching parties for office or material gain.

Under the 10th Schedule, there are four primary grounds for disqualification. First is the Voluntary Relinquishment of party membership, which the Supreme Court has interpreted broadly to include conduct that implies resignation. Second is Voting against the Whip, where a member defies the party's formal direction on the floor. Third applies to Independent Members who join a party post-election, and fourth to Nominated Members who join a party after six months of their term.

The 91st Amendment Shift:

Originally, the law allowed a "split" if 1/3rd of a party's members broke away. Recognizing that this facilitated mass defections, the 91st Amendment (2003) deleted the split provision. Today, only a Merger involving at least 2/3rds (66.6%) of the legislative party is recognized as a legal protection against disqualification.

2026 AAP-BJP Merger: A Legal Masterstroke

On April 24, 2026, the National Capital witnessed a political earthquake when seven out of ten Aam Aadmi Party (AAP) Rajya Sabha members officially joined the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP). Led by Raghav Chadha, this group utilized the mathematical precision of the 10th Schedule to execute what legal scholars call a "Wholesale Defection."

The "Merger Seven" Strategy

The group included high-profile names like Harbhajan Singh, Swati Maliwal, and Sandeep Pathak. By moving together as a block of seven, they hit exactly 70% of the party strength in the Rajya Sabha. Since 70% is greater than the required 66.6%, their action was classified as a merger of the legislative party, protecting them from the "Defection Trap."

Constitutional Validity

This move highlights the fundamental paradox of the law in 2026: while it successfully prevents individual members from switching sides ("Retail Defection"), it provides a legal highway for large groups to change the Rajya Sabha's power balance without seeking a fresh mandate from the electorate.

The Resignation-over-Defection Loophole

As we navigate 2026, the most significant challenge to the 10th Schedule is the Resignation Strategy. Instead of defecting and risking a long-term ban or disqualification, legislators simply resign from the House entirely.

This maneuver assists the opposing side by lowering the majority mark during a Trust Vote. The legislator then contests a by-election on the ticket of the new party. Constitutional experts argue that this subverts the 10th Schedule's spirit, as it essentially allows for a "deferred" defection.

The Presiding Officer as a Tribunal

The Presiding Officer (Speaker/Chairman) holds the ultimate power to disqualify. In the landmark Kihoto Hollohan (1992) case, the Supreme Court clarified that while the Speaker's decision is final, it is subject to Judicial Review because the Speaker acts as a "Tribunal" in these matters.

The Time-Limit Crisis

In 2026, the biggest legal debate revolves around the Speaker's tendency to delay disqualification petitions indefinitely. The Supreme Court in Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020) suggested a 3-month limit, but this remains a contested area where political bias often clashes with constitutional duty.

Neutrality Exception

Interestingly, the 10th Schedule does not apply to the Speaker themselves. If a member is elected Speaker and resigns from their party to maintain the House's dignity, they are exempt from disqualification—a rare provision honoring the neutrality of the Chair.
