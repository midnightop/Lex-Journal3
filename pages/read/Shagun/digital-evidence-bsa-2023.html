Digital Evidence under the BSA, 2023

Are Indian Courts Ready for the Digital Age?

Shagun Malik

IIMT University, Meerut

Phones, emails, social media chats, call records, CCTV footage and cloud storage have become central to how Indian litigation and criminal investigation actually run today. The Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 (BSA) responds to this shift through Sections 61, 62 and 63, which set out when and how electronic records can be treated as valid evidence.

This article's central argument: India is legally prepared for the digital age, but institutionally still catching up. That claim is tested across four indicators — legal, institutional, technological and constitutional readiness — rather than through a plain restatement of the statute.

Along the way, it traces the certificate mechanism under Section 63(4) from Anvar P.V. v P.K. Basheer through Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal to recent BSA-era rulings, including the Supreme Court's 2026 decision upholding the constitutionality of the hash-value and dual-signatory requirements in Pune Bar Association v Union of India.

What Counts as Digital Evidence

Digital evidence is any information with evidentiary value that is created, stored or transmitted digitally — emails, chats, photos, recordings, CCTV footage, call records, location data, browser history and metadata. The same message can leave traces on the sender's device, the recipient's device and a server all at once, and a photograph can carry creation and modification data invisible on the screen itself. Its value, in other words, often cannot be judged just by looking at what's on display.

A useful distinction runs through the rest of this piece: the device is only the medium. A phone is not itself the evidence — the messages, files and recordings it holds are. Sections 61 and 62 fold this material into the ordinary law of documentary evidence, but recognising that a digital record exists is not the same as trusting it. Because digital data can be copied invisibly, edited with software, or reconstructed after deletion, the real questions — authenticity, integrity, source, chain of custody — are answered by the conditions of proof in Section 63, not by Section 61's general recognition.

From Section 65B to Section 63

Under the old Indian Evidence Act, Sections 65A and 65B created a special mechanism for electronic records, and early courts disagreed on whether ordinary secondary-evidence rules could substitute for it. Anvar P.V. v P.K. Basheer (2014) settled that disagreement: a three-judge bench held electronic records offered as secondary evidence are governed exclusively by Section 65B, and the certificate was treated as a real safeguard of authenticity, not paperwork.

Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020) reaffirmed this and overruled a more relaxed line taken in Shafhi Mohammad, holding the certificate to be a condition precedent wherever Section 65B applies — with a narrow exception where the record sits with someone who can't be compelled to produce it.

The BSA carries this forward with two changes: Section 63(3) treats interconnected devices used for one activity as a single system for certification, and Section 63(4) adds a hash-value disclosure and a dual-signatory certificate — device custodian plus a qualified expert — replacing the old single-signatory model.

Section 63(4): The Certificate Requirement

Section 61 says an electronic record isn't inadmissible merely for being electronic. Section 62 sends proof of its contents to Section 63. And Section 63 is where the real work happens: computer output is deemed a document and admissible without the original, provided the conditions in Section 63(2) — regular system use, ordinary course of feeding data in, proper functioning, and output matching what was fed in — are met.

Section 63(4) requires a certificate each time the record is tendered: identifying it, describing how it was produced, disclosing its hash value, and signed by both the device custodian and an expert.

The Supreme Court tested this regime head-on in Pune Bar Association v Union of India (2026), where it was argued the dual-signatory, hash-value requirement was too heavy a burden on ordinary litigants. The Court upheld it — treating the hash value as a genuine anti-tampering safeguard rather than red tape — and clarified that suitably qualified private individuals, not only government-notified examiners, can sign as the expert.

But the certificate is a safeguard, not a substitute for scrutiny. It can confirm how a file was extracted without confirming who created the message inside it. Chandrabhan Sudam Sanap v State of Maharashtra (2025) shows the cost of getting this wrong: the Supreme Court set aside a conviction and death sentence because the prosecution never furnished the required certificate for CCTV footage placing the accused with the deceased — the footage was excluded from consideration entirely.

Primary vs Secondary — A Distinction Under Strain

Digital records don't fit neatly into "original" and "copy." A message on a phone might also live on a server or another authorised device, and identical copies can exist with none of the wear that usually marks a reproduction. Because a file can be duplicated without any visible loss, the more useful question is usually not whether something is technically an original, but whether it is authentic, complete, and reliably tied to its claimed source.

Original / primary evidence May be data within a system rather than one retrievable artefact (s 61)
Copy / secondary evidence Screenshots, printouts, exported chats, forensic copies (s 62)
Authentication Source, integrity, system functioning and metadata (s 63(2))
Certificate Mandatory dual-signatory, hash-value certificate (s 63(4)), upheld in Pune Bar Association

Screenshots and printouts make the stakes concrete: a screenshot can show a conversation without proving who created it or whether it's complete, and a printout strips away timestamps and metadata. In Kailas Bajirao Pawar v State of Maharashtra (2025), the Court held that video evidence satisfying Section 63 can be admitted without a written transcript and viewed directly by the court — though simply playing a disc in open court isn't, by itself, legal proof of its contents; the proper witness and procedure still matter.

Where the Real Difficulties Sit

Certification cannot repair a chain of custody that was broken before it began — preservation discipline has to start at seizure, not at the certificate stage. And proving a record is genuine isn't the same as proving who created it: a message from a number doesn't prove who was holding the phone, and a social account tied to one person may have been operated by someone else.

Metadata gets stripped away in ordinary copying; encryption protects privacy but can frustrate lawful access; and data sitting on cloud servers abroad raises cross-border cooperation questions that are legal rather than technical. None of this is solved by the certificate mechanism alone.

The sharpest new problem is generative AI. A deepfake recording can look and sound completely convincing while depicting something that never happened — which means visual plausibility is no longer a reliable test of genuineness. A certificate addressed to how a record was produced says nothing about whether its content was synthetically generated in the first place.

Digital Forensics: Necessary, Not Sufficient

Forensic imaging preserves the original device while examination happens on a copy; hash values let investigators confirm a copy matches its source at each stage. But hashing only proves consistency between copy and source — it doesn't prove the underlying information is true or who created it. It's an integrity check, not a full authentication method.

Expert evidence assists the court rather than replacing its judgment — judges still need to weigh methodology and reasoning, not just accept a conclusion, especially where experts disagree. And as AI-generated content gets harder to distinguish from the real thing through ordinary examination, no single detection technique can be treated as final; the response has to keep evolving rather than being a one-time fix.

So — Are Indian Courts Ready?

Measured against four indicators, the picture is mixed but clear:

Legal readiness is the strongest — Sections 61 to 63 give a structured, technology-neutral framework, tested and refined through Anvar, Arjun Panditrao Khotkar and now Pune Bar Association. Institutional readiness lags well behind — Sanap shows a case where the law was settled and the evidence was still lost because the certificate was simply never obtained; that's a failure of practice, not of statute. Technological readiness has to keep pace with encryption, cloud storage and, increasingly, deepfakes — a framework built around conventional computer output will strain as synthetic content gets harder to spot. Constitutional readiness asks whether evidence-gathering stays proportionate — a single seized device can hold years of communications mostly unconnected to the case, and efficiency cannot come at the cost of privacy safeguards.

Concluding Note

The BSA confirms that Indian evidence law has moved decisively past treating digital material as exceptional. Sections 61 to 63 provide a coherent, constitutionally sound framework, built on jurisprudence running consistently from Anvar through Sanap and Pawar. But legal recognition is only one indicator of readiness — and not the one India is currently weakest on. Forensic infrastructure, trained personnel and coordinated practice remain uneven; technology keeps outrunning any fixed baseline of preparedness; and constitutional discipline has to hold even as evidence-gathering gets more efficient. The question is no longer whether digital evidence belongs in the justice system — it clearly does — but whether the institutions handling it can keep pace with the technology producing it.
