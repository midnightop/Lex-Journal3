The Legal Gown Controversy: Does Mamata Banerjee Have the Right to Practice Law?

A Historic Appearance

On May 14, 2026, former West Bengal Chief Minister Mamata Banerjee made a dramatic return to the Calcutta High Court. Dressed in the full professional attire of an advocate, she appeared to argue a PIL, triggering an immediate legal firestorm.

Having served as the Chief Executive of the state for 15 consecutive years (2011–2026), Banerjee’s sudden appearance in lawyer’s robes—complete with the black coat and white bands—raised fundamental questions about the professional status of a constitutional office holder. Under Indian law, the credibility of the legal profession depends on practitioners being duly enrolled and authorized to act.

The visual of the veteran politician in the robes of a counsel immediately sparked a debate: Does 15 years of absence from active practice while holding a public salary automatically suspend a lawyer's license? Or can a former Chief Minister simply walk back into a courtroom and resume arguments without a formal nod from the Bar Council?

The BCI’s Fact-Finding Mission

The Bar Council of India (BCI), the national regulatory body for legal professionals, moved with unprecedented speed following the incident. In a formal directive, the BCI requested the West Bengal Bar Council to submit the enrollment and practice details of Mamata Banerjee within 48 hours.

The BCI has sought verification of five critical points:

  • The exact enrollment number and date of enrollment.
  • Whether her name is currently listed on the active "State Roll of Advocates."
  • Records of voluntary suspension or cessation of practice during her tenure as CM.
  • Whether an application for "Resumption of Practice" was submitted and accepted.
  • Current validity of the Certificate of Practice (COP).

The BCI has explicitly ordered that all original records be preserved without alteration. This inquiry is not just about one individual; it addresses the core of professional transparency and whether constitutional office holders have followed the same rules that govern every other legal practitioner in India.

The Advocates Act, 1961

The right to practice law in India is not a natural right but a statutory privilege governed by the Advocates Act, 1961. Section 29 of the Act states that there shall be only one class of persons entitled to practice the profession of law, namely, advocates.

When an advocate takes up a full-time salaried position or assumes a constitutional role that involves executive powers, they are generally expected to suspend their practice. Section 35 of the Act and the BCI Rules emphasize that a person cannot simultaneously engage in two professions or hold a position of profit while actively practicing law.

The Procedure for Resumption

If an advocate has suspended their practice for over a decade to serve as a Chief Minister, they cannot "automatically" resume practice. They must apply to the State Bar Council for resumption, pay the requisite fees, and demonstrate that they have not incurred any disqualification during their period of absence. Without the State Council's formal approval, donning the robes and appearing in court may be viewed as a violation of professional ethics.

The Sacred Robes: Dress Code Rules

The wearing of advocate robes is not merely a tradition; it is a legal requirement under Part VI, Chapter IV of the Bar Council of India Rules. The rules specify the exact type of coat, bands, and gown to be worn. However, these robes are reserved strictly for "practicing advocates."

"The credibility of the legal profession depends on every practitioner being duly enrolled and authorized to act." — BCI Principle.

If a person appears in court wearing advocate robes without having an active "Certificate of Practice," it is considered a serious breach of professional etiquette. The BCI is currently investigating whether Banerjee’s appearance in advocate robes, while the status of her enrollment was under question, undermines the sanctity of the courtroom dress code.
