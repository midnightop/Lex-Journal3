Mamata Banerjee Election Defeat 2026: Can Governor Remove CM If She Refuses to Resign?

Breaking Political & Constitutional Analysis · West Bengal 2026

Rey

In a stunning political earthquake that shook the foundations of Indian democracy, Mamata Banerjee's Trinamool Congress suffered a historic defeat in the 2026 West Bengal Assembly Elections — losing both the state majority and the Chief Minister's own constituency seat. What followed was constitutionally unprecedented: Banerjee publicly declared she would not resign. This article examines the legal scaffolding of the Indian Constitution, the Governor's powers under Articles 164 and 174, landmark judicial precedents, and what actually happened when the state's Assembly term expired on May 7, 2026.

1. Introduction: The Political Earthquake

The Mamata Banerjee election loss of May 2026 stands as one of the most dramatic political reversals in post-independence Indian history. For fifteen consecutive years, Banerjee and her All India Trinamool Congress (AITC/TMC) held an iron grip over West Bengal — a state of over 100 million people, the country's fourth most populous. The 2026 assembly elections shattered that dominance completely.

Following the declaration of results on May 4, 2026, Banerjee made an extraordinary statement at a press conference: "No question of me resigning. We were defeated not by public mandate but by conspiracy. I did not lose, I will not go to Raj Bhavan." The words ricocheted across India's political and constitutional landscape, triggering urgent debate among lawyers, constitutional scholars, and political analysts about what tools the state Governor and the Union Government possess when a sitting Chief Minister refuses to honour the democratic mandate.

The West Bengal election results 2026 were decisive by any metric. Voting was conducted in two phases — April 23 and April 29, 2026 — across all 294 assembly constituencies.

The Bharatiya Janata Party, led in the state by Suvendu Adhikari, swept 207 seats — comfortably crossing the majority mark of 147. The TMC, which had won 215 seats in 2021, was reduced to a rump of 80, a collapse of 134 seats.

This was not merely a loss of a seat; it stripped the Chief Minister of her very membership in the state legislature, with profound constitutional consequences.

Key Context: Why Bhabanipur Matters

Bhabanipur had been Banerjee's chosen "safe seat" since 2011. Losing it to the same opponent who had narrowly defeated her in 2021 — but this time with a larger margin — underscored the depth of the anti-incumbency wave sweeping even urban Kolkata.

3. Constitutional Duty After Losing Majority

India's constitutional framework is rooted in the Westminster model of parliamentary democracy. At its core lies the principle that the executive derives its authority from — and remains answerable to — the elected legislature. This relationship is codified in Article 164(2) of the Constitution: "The Council of Ministers shall be collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly of the State."

The corollary to collective responsibility is simple but absolute: if a government loses its majority — either in a floor test or through a general election — the constitutional obligation to resign arises immediately. This is not a courtesy; it is a constitutional imperative rooted in democratic legitimacy.

The "Caretaker" Convention

Under established constitutional convention, a government that has lost an election or a floor test may continue in office as a caretaker government — but only in a severely limited capacity. It is constitutionally prohibited from making major policy decisions, entering into new international agreements, making key appointments, or exercising any executive discretion that could bind the incoming government.

Constitutional Principle

A caretaker government governs by necessity, not by right. Its mandate is maintenance, not governance. Any executive action beyond routine administration by a defeated government is constitutionally vulnerable to challenge in a court of law.

4. What Happens If a CM Refuses to Resign?

The Mamata Banerjee resignation controversy escalated rapidly. Beyond her initial press conference statement, Banerjee reportedly doubled down in a closed-door party meeting on May 6, telling her colleagues: "Let them impose President's Rule if they want. Let them dismiss me if they want."

The Constitution of India anticipates this exact scenario and provides multiple escalating mechanisms for resolution: Step 1: The Floor Test, Step 2: Governor's Dismissal Power, and Step 3: Article 356 (President's Rule).

5. Governor's Powers Under Articles 164 & 174

Two constitutional provisions are central to understanding the Governor's powers in India in the context of a recalcitrant Chief Minister.

Article Provision
Art. 164(1) Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the Governor.
Art. 164(2) Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly.
Art. 174(2)(b) Governor may dissolve the Legislative Assembly. Invoked on May 7.

The "Pleasure of the Governor" Doctrine

The phrase "during the pleasure of the Governor" in Article 164(1) is deceptively powerful. As the Supreme Court held, the Governor cannot act arbitrarily — the pleasure must be exercised constitutionally and is triggered by loss of majority support.

6. Can the Governor Dismiss Mamata Banerjee?

Yes — but with significant procedural guardrails.

Constitutional law expert PDT Achary clarified: "Even if she doesn't resign, it doesn't matter... She can only stay the CM till May 6 by operation of the Constitution of India." Senior advocate Ram Jethmalani observed: "She's no longer the elected chief minister. She has to go anyway."

7. Historical Precedents & Legal Interpretation

S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994): This nine-judge Supreme Court bench ruling remains the foundational precedent. The Court held that a government's majority must be tested on the floor of the House — not assessed by the Governor privately.

The Article 164(4) Dimension: Even if she had remained Chief Minister beyond May 7, she had lost her seat in the Assembly. Under Article 164(4), a minister who is not a member of the state legislature for a consecutive period of six months automatically ceases to be a minister.

8. Political Impact on West Bengal

The TMC's defeat ends 15 consecutive years of Mamata Banerjee's rule. For the BJP, winning 207 seats represents the achievement of a decade-long political objective and a fundamental realignment of West Bengal politics. With 80 seats, the TMC remains a significant opposition force, but its future hinges on whether Mamata Banerjee can reinvent her political identity outside the government.

CONCLUSION: THE MANDATE PREVAILS

The Indian Constitution proved equal to the test. The answer, ultimately, was not provided by dramatic constitutional confrontation — but by the quiet, inexorable operation of Article 174 and the expiry of the Assembly's term on May 7, 2026. Mamata Banerjee did not resign. She did not need to: the Constitution itself ended her tenure as Chief Minister at midnight on that date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the Governor remove a Chief Minister in India?

Yes. Under Article 164(1), if a CM loses majority support, the Governor may withdraw pleasure and dismiss the government. However, a floor test is the preferred mechanism.

What happened when Mamata Banerjee refused to resign?

Her refusal became constitutionally irrelevant. The Assembly's five-year term expired on May 7, 2026. Governor RN Ravi issued a formal dissolution order under Article 174(2)(b).
